There is something satisfying about a small loop: try something, notice what happened, try again. It is a useful place to start thinking about both learning and the systems we build to support it.

This notebook entry uses a deliberately tiny example. Imagine two answers to the same question. One is short and incomplete; the other is thorough and a little wordy. Which one should our system prefer?

## First, decide what matters

The answer depends on what we choose to reward. A reward is a compressed statement of our preferences, and compression leaves things out.

> A number can tell us which answer won. It cannot, by itself, tell us whether we asked the right question.

Our toy scores look like this:

| Answer | Brevity | Completeness |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Short answer | 0.95 | 0.40 |
| Detailed answer | 0.50 | 0.90 |

These numbers are invented for the demonstration. They are not measurements of any model.

## Turn the dial

Try changing how much weight we give completeness. Neither answer changes, but our preference does.

## Make the choice explicit

The calculation is intentionally simple:

```python title="reward.py" showLineNumbers

def reward(brevity, completeness, weight=0.5):
    return (1 - weight) * brevity + weight * completeness

short_answer = reward(0.95, 0.40)
detailed_answer = reward(0.50, 0.90)
```

Writing this down makes a hidden decision visible. We can discuss it, change it, and check where it behaves badly.

<details>
<summary>What does this example leave out?</summary>

Real evaluation has more dimensions: correctness, the user's needs, the difficulty of the task, and whether a response is useful at all. Combining two made-up scores is only a way to explore one tradeoff. It is not a recipe for a production reward function.

</details>

## Back to the learner

The interesting question is not just whether feedback exists. It is what the feedback makes worth doing.

Before making the loop faster, I would ask three questions:

1. What behavior do we actually want?
2. What proxy are we measuring instead?
3. Where could those two come apart?

Small loops make those questions easier to see.
