On November 2, [SemiAnalysis](https://newsletter.semianalysis.com/p/gdpx-measuring-frontier-model-capabilities) released [GDPx](https://gdpx.semianalysis.com/), a benchmark that measures agentic capability across the domains where frontier intelligence is worth the most. [Chakra](https://www.chakra.dev/) was the proprietary data partner for the legal domain, and I led that work on our side.

The headline: **the best model scores 12%.**

This post is my version of the story. What GDPx is, how the legal tasks were built, and the one result I keep thinking about.

# What GDPx is

GDPx covers 11 domains: chip design, cybersecurity, biology, finance, legal, quant trading, recursive self-improvement, hardware design, materials science, alignment, and software engineering. The tasks come from 14 specialized data partners, and each domain counts equally toward the headline number.

A few design choices I like:

1. **Long horizons.** The default time limit is 8 hours per task.
1. **Native harnesses.** Models run in their own agent harness where one exists.
1. **A hard headline metric.** The score is pass@1 over three attempts.

Claude Opus 5.5 was the best all-around model. GPT-6 Astra led recursive self-improvement and materials science. Gemini 4 Carbon was the strongest in biology. Nobody is close to done.

# The legal track

Chakra supplied all of the legal tasks. They span eight practice areas:

- M&A, venture capital, and capital markets
- Corporate governance
- Intellectual property and data privacy
- Administrative law and dispute resolution

And six kinds of work: legal research, drafting, redlining, term extraction, issue spotting, and risk assessment.

The tasks were written by practicing lawyers from our expert roster, which includes senior partners at large US firms, start-up general counsels, Fortune 500 commercial counsels, and heads of legal operations at asset managers. Each author starts from an assignment drawn from their own practice.

An environment is a folder of Word and PDF files: agreements, term sheets, offering documents, court filings, and emails from a client or a partner. Some of it is built from public SEC EDGAR filings. The agent gets the folder and an instruction, the way a junior associate would.

The expert also writes two more things:

1. **The rubric** the verifier grades against.
1. **A reference solution** showing one valid way to do the task.

# Trusting the grader

A legal benchmark is only as good as its judge. Three checks we ran before relying on any score:

1. **Judge error against expert labels.** If the judge and the lawyer disagree on a criterion, the judge is wrong until proven otherwise.
1. **Five gradings per output**, tracking verdicts at the criterion level so we can see which criteria are noisy.
1. **Judge-model ablations.** Hold the rubric and the outputs fixed, swap the grading model, and see what moves.

If each grading of a criterion is an independent draw that agrees with the expert with probability $q$, the majority of five is right with probability

$$
P(\text{majority correct}) = \sum_{k=3}^{5} \binom{5}{k} q^{k} (1-q)^{5-k}
$$

At $q = 0.9$ that is about $0.991$. The real world is less kind, because judge errors are correlated. That is exactly why the ablations matter.

# The redline nobody passed

The public legal task is `quillon-series-b-redline`. The agent represents the lead investor in a Series B and has to redline a draft of the amended and restated certificate of incorporation.

Legal was meant to be graded pass/fail, because a redline is either something you can send or it isn't. Across 20 rollouts from four models, **no redline was usable**. Binary score: 0% for everyone.

So GDPx also reports partial credit:

| Model          | Mean partial score |
| -------------- | ------------------ |
| Claude Opus 5.5 | 44%               |
| GPT-6 Astra    | 25%                |
| Gemini 4 Argon | 2%                 |
| GLM-5.3        | 0.5%               |

The best single run reached 57%. Two models landed near zero because the rubric penalizes re-trading terms that were already agreed, and those penalties wiped out the credit they earned.

The failures trace to three causes.

**1. Not reading the documents together.** In 13 of 20 runs, the agent missed what the cap table implied: the other shareholders hold enough votes to strip the client's protections.

**2. Not anticipating the workaround.** In 20 of 20 runs, the redline left the company free to act through a subsidiary and bypass the charter's protective provisions.

**3. Treating the client's email as the source of truth.** In 14 of 20 runs, the agent applied what the client asked for without flagging that it re-trades terms already signed in the term sheet.

The third one is my favorite, because it is a sycophancy failure wearing a suit. A good associate pushes back: "we can ask for this, but we agreed to the opposite last week." The models just did what the latest message said.

# What I took away

**Binary grading is honest.** Partial credit is useful for telling models apart today. But the client's question is "can I send this?", and right now the answer is no.

**The hard part is the folder.** None of the three failures is about legal knowledge. Each one is about holding several documents in mind and noticing where they disagree.

**Experts are the bottleneck, and they should be.** The task, the rubric, and the reference solution all come from someone who does this work for a living. There is no shortcut around that.
